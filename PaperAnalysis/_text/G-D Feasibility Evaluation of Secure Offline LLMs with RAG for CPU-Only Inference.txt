                           progressively more accurate decisions as they process more information during training and
                           fine-tuning [1]. One tool driving AI advancements is the development of language models.
                           Language models are computational systems designed to produce human-like language
                           and developed using mathematical frameworks that include parameters to predict word
                           sequences accurately [2]. While many are generative and designed to output sequences
                           of text, others perform tasks like classification or retrieval using similar probabilistic
                           frameworks. Generative models manage the relationships between words and phrases
                           through transformers, a type of neural network architecture that encodes text into numerical
                           representations by applying attention mechanisms to assign weighted values based on
                           context [3]. These models generate text based on patterns learned during training. However,
                           their responses may occasionally deviate from natural human speech due to inherent
                           limitations in the underlying mathematical structure and training data. Continuous training
                           and fine-tuning help improve the coherence and accuracy of generated text, making it more
                           closely resemble human communication. Language models can also be specialized for
                           specific domains and applications and are increasingly employed to answer a wide range
                           of user queries and assist with diverse tasks.

                                 Despite the rapid advancement of large language models, their use in secure, resource-
                           constrained environments such as government offices remains limited. Many available
                           solutions depend on cloud infrastructure or GPU-accelerated systems, which are often
                           incompatible with government computing environments due to security, cost, or procure-
                           ment restrictions. This presents a critical gap in understanding how language models can
                           be deployed effectively on CPU-only systems that operate offline. The central problem
                           addressed in this research is whether open-source language models, when combined with
                           retrieval-augmented generation techniques, can support essential tasks such as question
                           answering and summarization on commonly available hardware without compromising
                           performance, accuracy, or data privacy.

                           Objectives

                                 This study addresses the challenge by evaluating the feasibility of using various large
                           language models on consumer-grade hardware, with a particular emphasis on models
                           optimized for CPU-based performance rather than GPU-based systems. To achieve this
                           goal, the following specific objectives were established:

                           · Identify and apply appropriate model evaluation metrics.
                           · Assess a range of models using the selected metrics.
                           · Develop a methodology to test text summarization and question-answering models.
                           · Develop code to test and evaluate models.
                           · Recommend the most effective text summarization and question-answering models

                                 based on performance.

                                 One way to categorize current large language models (LLM) offerings is by distin-
                           guishing between cloud-based and locally deployed models, each with unique advantages
                           and limitations. These models have a wide range of possible applications, and their perfor-
                           mance can be enhanced through various techniques, such as domain-specific fine-tuning
                           and retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Domain-specific fine-tuning involves adjusting
                           models to perform optimally in specialized areas of knowledge, while RAG integrates
                           external data sources during the generation process to improve the accuracy and rele-
                           vance of responses. Although both methods offer significant advantages, this study will
                           focus on RAG due to its ability to dynamically incorporate relevant external information,
                           which can improve accuracy and reduce hallucinations rather than directly enhancing
                           model performance.

                           2. Materials and Methods

                                 The language models were selected based on their open-source availability, ability
                           to function securely offline, and compatibility with consumer-grade hardware. The hard-
                           ware used in this study includes a high-end workstation and a standard consumer laptop,
                           selected both for their availability and for their relevance to real-world deployment sce-
                           narios. These CPU-only configurations were intentionally chosen to reflect the limitations
                           commonly found in government office environments, where computer systems are typi-
                           cally not equipped with dedicated GPUs due to cost, security, or procurement constraints.
                           Additionally, the study details the evaluation metrics, including accuracy, efficiency, and
                           summarization capabilities, providing a comprehensive overview of how these models
                           perform in diverse computational environments. This approach enables a practical assess-
                           ment of deploying advanced language models efficiently, even in settings with limited
                           computational resources.

                           2.1. Framework Selection

                                 Five tools for hosting LLMs locally were evaluated: LocalGPT [4], PrivateGPT [5],
                           GPT4All [6], llama.cpp [7], and Ollama [8]. Each tool was assessed based on several factors,
                           including compatibility with typical government-approved operating systems (Windows
                           and Ubuntu). Therefore, the tools were assessed not only for performance but also for
                           their ability to function offline after initial setup, native support for RAG, and ease of use
                           without requiring extensive programming expertise.

                                 It is important to note that this study did not aim to build a custom retrieval-augmented
                           generation system by separately configuring inference backends and RAG frameworks.
                           Instead, the goal was to evaluate complete, open-source tools that could be deployed
                           with minimal setup. While modular separation between inference engines and RAG
                           layers is common in large-scale production environments, this study prioritized integrated
                           solutions in order to focus on the real-world usability and performance of established
                           open-source packages that do not require significant development effort. This approach
                           reflects the practical needs of organizations operating in secure or resource-constrained
                           environments, where technical staff may not have the capacity to implement or maintain
                           custom pipeline architectures.

                                 While llama.cpp and Ollama were both considered, ultimately, neither was selected
                           for this study. llama.cpp is a low-level C++ implementation originally designed for running
                           LLaMA models on consumer-grade hardware and has since expanded to support a broader
                           range of models [7]. Ollama builds on llama.cpp and simplifies model loading and inference
                           with additional optimizations [8]. However, neither tool provides native support for
                           retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines. Implementing custom RAG functionality
                           would have required extensive integration work, such as binding inference engines with
                           separate retrieval and chunking frameworks. Because this study aimed to evaluate out-
                           of-the-box open-source tools with minimal development overhead, llama.cpp and Ollama
                           were excluded.

                                 LocalGPT, developed by PromptEngineer, offers an integrated RAG setup, aligning
                           with the project's emphasis on practical deployment rather than system-level toolchain
                           development. It provides efficient inference performance and native RAG capabilities,
                           and it is compatible with both Windows and Ubuntu operating systems [4]. It also allows
                           the integration of various language models and supports ingestion from various docu-
                           ment types, including DOCX, PDFs, spreadsheets, and Python code. Marketed as a RAG
                           pipeline, LocalGPT operates entirely within the local environment, ensuring that data never
                           leaves the user's system while maintaining performance [4]. LocalGPT supports multiple
                           hardware configurations, including NVIDIA GPUs, Apple Silicon CPUs, and traditional
Information 2025, 16, 744                                                                                                                                              4 of 23

                           x86 CPUs, making it accessible to users with limited resources. The software includes
                           features like the ability to save chat conversations, utilize context history, and display
                           cited sources, which are essential functions for ensuring effective RAG operation and data
                           retrieval accuracy [4].

                                 LocalGPT builds upon PrivateGPT by improving performance across platforms while
                           maintaining the same focus on privacy and offline operation. It also enhances document
                           processing capabilities with more efficient handling of larger datasets and improved RAG
                           functionalities. In contrast, PrivateGPT lacked some of the more advanced RAG features
                           necessary for the project's requirements, and GPT4All showed limitations in handling
                           document types effectively, leading to its exclusion. As a result, LocalGPT was selected
                           because it met all the project criteria including compatibility, performance, and ease of
                           integration into the project environment.

                           2.2. Hardware Selection

                                 Although both devices used to test the language models are accessible to typical
                           consumers, their capacities and specifications differ significantly. The first device, an HP
                           EliteBook 840 G5, is a laptop with 16 GB of RAM and an Intel Core i7-8650U processor,
                           commonly found in mainstream consumer laptops. This CPU operates at a base frequency
                           of 1.90 GHz and includes four cores [9]. The selection of this device simulates the experience
                           of a typical consumer, such as a student or researcher, who may wish to use a lightweight
                           RAG system for private document queries. The second device, an HP Z820 Workstation,
                           is a desktop computer equipped with 128 GB of RAM and powered by two Intel Xeon
                           E5-2667v2 CPUs typically used in server environments. Each CPU operates at a base
                           frequency of 3.30 GHz and features eight cores [10]. This configuration represents the
                           higher-end of consumer hardware, offering substantial processing power and speed, ideal
                           for machine learning tasks requiring intensive computation.

                                 Neither device employed a GPU, mirroring the hardware constraints found in many
                           public sector or government office environments. This choice allowed for a more realistic
                           assessment of LLM performance in secure, offline deployments where data privacy is
                           paramount and high-end graphical processing units are often unavailable or unauthorized.
                           It is worth mentioning that language model performance on a GPU can be up to 100 times
                           faster than on a CPU, assuming the GPU has sufficient VRAM [11]. A minor considera-
                           tion in this experiment is the operating system used on each device. One computer ran
                           Windows 11, while the other ran Ubuntu 22.04. Nevertheless, both systems deployed a
                           virtual environment provided by Anaconda, ensuring that any differences in performance
                           attributable to the operating system should be negligible.

                           2.3. Model Selection

                                 The selection of language models was a critical aspect of optimizing the RAG system
                           for question-answering and summarization tasks. Several models were considered for
                           testing. However, each selected model had to meet a few essential requirements. First,
                           the models needed to be available for local use with accessible pre-trained weights and
                           licensing terms that allowed evaluation and redistribution. Although the term "open
                           source" is often used broadly, it is important to recognize that openness in AI involves
                           multiple factors, including access to training data, fine-tuning code, documentation, and
                           permissive licensing for commercial use. For the purposes of this study, models were
                           classified as open source if they provided downloadable weights, community-maintained
                           support, and licenses that permitted local deployment for research or non-commercial
                           applications. This working definition ensured that the selected models could be integrated
                           into an offline, CPU-only environment with transparency and reproducibility.

                                 Second, the models needed to function securely offline. This requirement was vital
                           for replicating secure deployment scenarios commonly encountered in defense or health-
                           care applications. In many of these environments, especially within government offices,
                           internet access may be restricted or monitored, and cloud-based tools are often prohibited.
                           Therefore, the selected models had to support isolated operation on local CPUs without
                           sacrificing reliability or performance. Lastly, the models had to be lightweight enough to
                           run efficiently on consumer-grade hardware, particularly on modest CPUs. This aspect
                           was critical to assess the feasibility of deploying language models on commonly accessible
                           consumer hardware. Finally, the chosen models needed to strike a balance between having
                           sufficient parameters to ensure accurate responses while not exceeding computational
                           resources or user response expectations.

                           2.4. Resource Selection

                                 The selection of the source material for testing the language models was based on
                           several important considerations. First, the source material needed to be in a widely
                           accessible format, such as PDF, to ensure compatibility with the RAG pipeline's document
                           ingestion process. Additionally, the source had to contain clear, well-defined information
                           that the selected language models could search for and retrieve. The material also needed
                           to be sufficiently large to support multiple queries based on the content. Finally, the source
                           had to primarily consist of text, as text-heavy content would allow for more efficient testing
                           of smaller language models, which might struggle with complex visual data like figures
                           or charts.

                                 Given these criteria, a college textbook was deemed the most suitable choice, as
                           textbooks typically provide clear explanations and contain extensive amounts of textual
                           information. A textbook with minimal reliance on figures was also prioritized to avoid
                           confusing smaller models. The selected textbook was Project Management for Engineering,
                           Business, and Technology (5th Edition) by John M. Nicholas and Herman Steyn [22]. This
                           1389-page textbook offers comprehensive coverage of business and management concepts,
                           particularly for individuals starting careers as managers and leaders in STEM fields [22].
                           It is clear and structured content made it an ideal resource for testing the selected LLMs,
                           allowing them to work efficiently across each chapter. The textbook was chosen not only
                           for its clarity and extensive textual content, but also because the research team had prior
                           experience with the material, and it was already available in a format compatible with the
                           pipeline. In addition, its content could be referenced in a reproducible manner without
                           legal or ethical ambiguity, making it a practical and academically appropriate choice for
                           this study. Importantly, while this textbook was selected specifically for its structural
                           applicability to this research, any textbook or document meeting similar criteria could be
                           used for replication, provided that proper considerations for publishing and copyright
                           compliance are observed.

                           2.6. Metric Selection

                                 Metrics are critical in evaluating the effectiveness of language models, particularly
                           when comparing their performance. This research focuses on measuring the capabilities
                           of a RAG application to provide accurate answers and summaries while also promoting
                           efficient, concise responses. Additionally, speed metrics play an important role, offering
                           insights into the performance of LLMs across different types of hardware.

                                 Latency, an essential aspect of LLM inference performance, helps determine how
                           effectively a model operates in real-world scenarios by measuring how quickly it can deliver
                           responses [23]. As this research is not focused on throughput (the number of requests
                           a model can handle simultaneously), this metric solely focuses on latency. Latency was
                           measured in 3 parts: (1) the time taken to process a prompt; (2) the time taken to generate a
                           response; and (3) per-token latency, calculated by total time/tokens used. These metrics
                           offer insights into how factors like the number of parameters and level of quantization
                           impact model efficiency. LocalGPT recorded all latency data, facilitating tracking of results.

                                 To assess the quality of model outputs, both statistical and model-based evaluation
                           metrics were used. Statistical methods compare a reference passage to the generated text
                           using metrics such as precision and recall. Common tools like Recall-Oriented Understudy
                           for Gisting Evaluation (ROUGE) [24], Bilingual Evaluation Understudy (BLEU) [25], and
                           F1 score are widely adopted but often fail to capture nuanced semantic differences [26].
                           To address these limitations, this study selected Metric for Evaluation of Translation with
                           Explicit Ordering (METEOR) and BERTScore for primary use.

                                 METEOR improves upon traditional statistical methods by normalizing words to
                           their base form and using a database of synonyms, reducing the penalties for minor
                           word variations [27]. However, as a statistical method, it may still penalize models that
                           do not perfectly match the reference text, even when the semantic meaning matches.
                           BERTScore leverages pre-trained language models and computes the cosine similarity
                           between the contextual embeddings of words in the reference and generated texts [28]. This
                           approach allows for better recognition of contextual meaning, reducing penalties for word
                           choice variations while incorporating classical statistical measures like recall and F1 score.
                           Together, these two metrics provide a comprehensive evaluation, balancing the precision of
                           statistical methods with the contextual understanding offered by model-based approaches.

                           2.7. Implementation

                                 The implementation process began with the setup of all necessary software com-
                           ponents. LocalGPT, an open-source RAG framework, was used to manage document
                           ingestion, vectorization, and inference. The project repository was cloned from https:
                           //github.com/PromtEngineer/localGPT (accessed on 15 July 2024) and configured in a
                           Python 3.10 Conda environment on both Windows 11 and Ubuntu 22.04 systems. Required
                           dependencies were installed using the requirements.txt file provided in the repository.
                           LocalGPT was run in CPU-only mode, with GGUF-format models placed in the designated
                           model directory. No additional components were added or modified beyond what was
                           required to ingest and query the study's source material. The RAG system followed Local-
                           GPT's default configuration, which includes FAISS as the vector database, cosine similarity
                           for retrieval, and a sliding window chunking strategy with a chunk size of 512 tokens and an
                           overlap of 64 tokens. Once the environment was prepared, the source textbook was ingested
                           and the evaluation questions were finalized. Formal testing was performed using the four
                           models shown in Table 1, focused on performance and resource efficiency. Each model was
                           given the questions in Table 2 ten times, metrics were calculated, and statistics were recorded.

                                 During testing, no other applications or background processes were run to preserve tim-
                           ing accuracy. Output was logged from the terminal and saved as .txt files. A Jupyter notebook
                           was used to organize results and extract response and timing data using Python libraries
                           including Pandas (version 2.2.2) and Matplotlib (version 3.9.1). Model outputs were scored
                           using METEOR and BERTScore against the reference excerpts shown in Appendix A. All
                           evaluations were performed using automated metrics; no manual grading was conducted.

                                 All models were executed via the LocalGPT interface using llama-cpp-python as
                           the inference backend. Inference was performed in CPU-only mode with default multi-
                           threading enabled; no manual tuning of parallelism parameters (e.g., thread count or
                           batching) was applied. Additionally, all models were evaluated using identical inference
                           parameters: temperature = 0.2 and repetition_penalty = 1.15. Questions were submitted
                           as individual prompts in a fixed order during a single session for each model, reflecting
                           typical offline usage scenarios. This design ensured consistency and reduced variability,
Information 2025, 16, 744                                                                                                                                              9 of 23

                           although fixed ordering and same-session prompting may introduce minor order effects.
                           These considerations are addressed in the Section 4.

                           3. Results

                                 The results here reflect one complete evaluation cycle for each language model across
                           two hardware platforms. All models were tested using a fixed set of ten questions, responses
                           judged by METEOR and BERTScore metrics for answer quality, capturing both lexical
                           and contextual similarity. This dual-reference approach allowed for evaluating factual
                           accuracy against source material and estimating summarization quality against a strong
                           commercial benchmark.

                                 Multiple trial runs were conducted to ensure the testing environment was stable
                           and consistent. While the processing times for generating results may vary slightly even
                           on the same device, the scoring remained consistent across all tests. Overall, the results
                           indicate that specific models demonstrated a superior ability to answer the questions,
                           and the workstation, equipped with higher-performance hardware, generated responses
                           significantly faster than the laptop.

                                 While Sections 3.1 and 3.2 present observable differences in model behavior across
                           tasks and hardware configurations, the intent of this study is not to rank models by
                           performance. Instead, these results offer practical insight into how well various open-
                           source models function under CPU-only, offline conditions using retrieval-augmented
                           generation. The evaluation highlights strengths and limitations relevant to real-world
                           deployment, supporting the central aim of this feasibility study: determining whether
                           locally hosted language models can provide reliable answers and summaries in secure,
                           resource-constrained environments.

                           4. Discussion

                                 Several inferences can be drawn from the results of these simulations, and a notable
                           aspect is the effectiveness of different evaluation metrics. As discussed earlier, the METEOR
                           metric proved inconsistent in evaluating summarization accuracy, primarily due to its lack
                           of contextual understanding and reliance on lexical overlap. This inconsistency was evident
                           in the lower and more variable METEOR scores on Q6­10. While METEOR offered some
                           insight into performance trends, it did not reliably distinguish among models in complex
                           tasks. Fortunately, BERTScore provided more stable and interpretable results, particularly
                           for factual question answering (Q1­5). In the BERTScore analysis, Question 10 consistently
                           yielded the highest scores across all models, further indicating that it was the most accessible
                           summarization prompt.

                                 Due to the integration of RAG, which allowed each model to extract relevant infor-
                           mation directly from the textbook, all models demonstrated strong performance on direct
                           question answering tasks (Q1­5). This was reflected in high BERTScore and METEOR
                           values when responses were compared to direct textbook excerpts as reference answers.
                           The LLaMA models in particular showed consistent semantic alignment across all factual
                           questions, with Model 2 achieving the highest average BERTScore and METEOR perfor-
                           mance on both hardware platforms. These results indicate that, when supported by reliable
                           document retrieval, even smaller language models running on consumer-grade CPUs, can
                           produce accurate, consistent, and contextually aligned answers to direct questions.

                                 However, summarization posed a greater challenge. While the models could locate
                           relevant information, they frequently failed to generate coherent or complete chapter sum-
                           maries. Several responses were vague, omitted required elements like chapter titles, or
                           declined to provide summaries altogether. In many of these cases the output logs showed
                           the retrieval process returned chunks that did not match the query or failed to return any
                           chunks. These issues suggest that limited context windows and memory constraints re-
                           duced the models' ability to handle long, generative tasks effectively. While RAG improved
                           factual retrieval, it did not compensate for broader summarization limitations.
Information 2025, 16, 744                                                                                                                                             13 of 23

                                 Another finding relates to the relationship between speed and accuracy on the worksta-
                           tion and the laptop--as expected the difference in hardware did not impact the quality of the
                           language models' responses. However, the amount of computational resources influenced the
                           time required to load the models, assess the prompts, and generate responses. For example,
                           the average prompt assessment time on the laptop was approximately four times longer than
                           on the workstation (88 s versus 23 s). Across all models the laptop had evaluation times that
                           were 3 to 6 times slower, which is an important consideration when developing applications
                           for different use cases. While results from the workstation and laptop are presented sepa-
                           rately, the purpose is to illustrate performance variability across two representative CPU-only
                           environments, not to generalize across all possible hardware configurations.

                                 Two limitations in the evaluation design should be noted. All questions were submit-
                           ted in a fixed order during a single session for each model, which may introduce minor
                           order effects or session-level context influence. While this approach reflects common usage
                           patterns for offline deployments, it does not eliminate the possibility of sequencing bias.
                           A project management professional evaluated all prompts, reference excerpts and one
                           exam, and judged them to be reasonable. However, they did not review all 100 responses
                           from each of the four models--we used text similarity metrics. As a result, this may limit
                           interpretability in edge cases where nuanced judgment would typically be required.

                                 The primary goal of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of running open-source
                           LLMs with RAG on CPU-only systems in secure, offline environments, not to establish
                           statistically significant differences between models. The observations throughout Section 4
                           contribute to this aim by identifying which implementation conditions, hardware configura-
                           tions, and model characteristics influence practical usability and performance. While some
                           findings reflect narrow differences, these details inform deployment decisions where trade-
                           offs between speed, accuracy, and resource usage are critical. For instance, the variability in
                           summarization quality and timing behavior across models reveals potential limitations for
                           certain applications, while the consistent success in direct question answering reinforces
                           confidence in using these systems for structured query tasks. Collectively, these results
                           offer guidance for selecting models under real-world constraints, even if broader theoretical
                           generalizations are beyond the scope of this feasibility-focused evaluation.

                           5. Conclusions

                                 This study assessed the feasibility and performance of locally hosted large language
                           models using retrieval-augmented generation on consumer-grade hardware for question-
                           answering and summarization tasks. By employing LocalGPT as an interface, four open-
                           source models were evaluated: Phi-3-Mini-4k-Instruct, Llama 2 Chat 7B, Mistral 7B Instruct
                           v0.2, and Llama 2 Chat 13B, on both a high-powered workstation and a standard consumer-
                           grade laptop. The findings demonstrate that modestly sized, locally hosted LLMs can
                           provide accurate and semantically aligned answers to direct factual questions when inte-
                           grated with RAG, even on devices with limited computational resources. While models
                           differed in timing efficiency, output variability, and alignment with reference answers, all
                           exhibited baseline competency in factual retrieval and question answering under CPU-only
                           constraints. With a focus on identifying practical constraints, benefits, and trade-offs rele-
                           vant to real-world deployment scenarios, these findings underscore the practical viability
                           of deploying LLMs offline for structured query tasks in resource-limited environments.

                                 Despite their strengths in direct factual answering, the models showed limitations
                           in summarization tasks, particularly when required to condense entire chapters from the
                           source material. The summarization capabilities were inconsistent, and the models often
                           failed to produce accurate or comprehensive summaries. This suggests that while RAG en-
Information 2025, 16, 744                                                                                                                                             14 of 23

                           hances the models' ability to retrieve specific information, processing and summarizing large
                           text volumes remains challenging for smaller LLMs in resource-constrained environments.

                                 The scope of this research was constrained by several practical factors. Only CPU-
                           based inference was explored, which, while aligned with typical government and resource-
                           restricted environments, does not capture the full performance potential of these models
                           with GPU acceleration. The models selected were intentionally smaller and older, prioritiz-
                           ing known stability, community support, and compatibility with offline environments. This
                           decision limited the evaluation of newer, potentially more capable models. Furthermore,
                           the study relied on a single source document and a fixed set of 10 questions, which may
                           not generalize to all use cases. Restricted internet access, while necessary for simulating
                           secure deployments, also prevented integration with more dynamic RAG configurations
                           that rely on live document retrieval.

                                 Based on the results, the deployment of small to mid-sized open-source LLMs is
                           feasible for secure, offline environments where direct question answering is the primary
                           need. For organizations with limited computing infrastructure, especially those relying on
                           CPU-only systems, quantized models and efficient RAG pipelines like LocalGPT present
                           practical utility. However, caution is advised when using these models for complex summa-
                           rization tasks unless fine-tuning or supplementary processing is applied. Future research
                           may benefit from evaluating broader document sets, more diverse query types, and newer
                           model architectures. Incorporating GPU-based testing would also provide a more complete
                           performance profile for organizations with greater hardware flexibility. To the authors'
                           knowledge, this is one of the first empirical studies to evaluate open-source RAG pipelines
                           for local LLM inference in offline or privacy-sensitive deployments.

                                 This study offers a practical roadmap for organizations, particularly in the public
                           sector, that seek to adopt language model technologies without compromising data security.
                           It demonstrates that CPU-only systems are capable of supporting useful LLM applications
                           without the need for expensive hardware upgrades. Additionally, it supports the use of
                           locally hosted models as a way to reduce privacy risks associated with cloud-based AI
                           platforms, providing a secure alternative for processing sensitive or proprietary informa-
                           tion. These findings may help guide procurement, deployment, and policy decisions in
                           environments where secure, autonomous AI systems are needed.
